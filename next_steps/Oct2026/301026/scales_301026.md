# Why standardising changes the picture (30 October 2026)

The two figures `../../../slides/281026/figures/population_constants_not_standardized.png` and
`population_constants_standardized.png` show the same eleven graphs, the same $\varepsilon$ grid and the same three
constants, yet they look nothing alike: not standardised, the lasso's conditions hold near the diagonal and
$\rho_w$ rises in steps; standardised, $\rho\lt1$ never holds, $\rho_{\mathrm{weak}}$ sits at $1$ from the start and
$\rho_w$ is positive from $\varepsilon=0.02$ on. This note explains why. Code: `rho_w_anatomy.py`; tests:
`tests/test_rho_w_anatomy_301026.py`. The companion note [rho_w_steps_301026.md](rho_w_steps_301026.md) takes the
$\rho_w$ curves apart feature by feature.

**The short answer.** Standardising is an exact change of variables: it maps every exact fit of the Lyapunov equation
to an exact fit, so the model, its support and the set of competing exact fits are the same on both scales. What it
changes is the price of each entry in the $\ell_1$ penalty: the entry $M_{ij}$ is charged $\mathrm{sd}_j/\mathrm{sd}_i$
times as much after standardising. In the Lyapunov equation an edge $t\to h$ and its reverse $h\to t$ trade against each
other at the rate $\Sigma_{tt}/\Sigma_{hh}$, the ratio of the two variances; on the correlation scale that ratio is
$1$ for every pair and every $\varepsilon$. So standardising puts every edge on a first-order tie with its reverse. All
the differences between the two figures follow from that one fact.

## 1. What the estimator sees on each scale

| | not standardised (`cov`) | standardised (`corr`, the campaign) |
|---|---|---|
| data | $X$ with covariance $\Sigma^\ast=\Sigma(M^\ast,2I)$ | $\tilde X=D^{-1}X$, $D=\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{sd}_1,\dots,\mathrm{sd}_p)$ |
| covariance in the loss | $\Sigma^\ast$ | $R=D^{-1}\Sigma^\ast D^{-1}=\mathrm{corr}(\Sigma^\ast)$ |
| $C$ in the loss | $2I$ | $\tilde C=D^{-1}(2I)D^{-1}=2D^{-2}$ |
| the truth it is compared with | $M^\ast$ | $\tilde M^\ast=D^{-1}M^\ast D$, $\tilde M_{ij}=M_{ij}\,\mathrm{sd}_j/\mathrm{sd}_i$ |
| penalty | $\lambda\lVert M\rVert_1$ | $\lambda\lVert\tilde M\rVert_1=\lambda\sum_{ij}\lvert M_{ij}\rvert\,\mathrm{sd}_j/\mathrm{sd}_i$ |

![the matrices of the chain at eps = 1](figures/scales_matrices.png)

For the chain $1\to2\to3$ at $\varepsilon=1$ (figure): the variances are $(1,\,0.667,\,0.383)$, falling along the
chain because $d_i=1+\mathrm{depth}(i)$ makes downstream nodes revert faster. The standardised truth has
$\tilde M_{21}=1.225$ and $\tilde M_{32}=1.319$ instead of $1$, and the weight matrix $\omega_{ij}=\mathrm{sd}_j/\mathrm{sd}_i$
is below $1$ above the diagonal and above $1$ below it: on this chain the standardised lasso charges the true edges
(below the diagonal) *more* and the reverses *less*, by exactly the factor that cancels the variance advantage of
Section 3.

## 2. What is preserved: the exact fits

With $\tilde M=D^{-1}MD$, $R=D^{-1}\Sigma D^{-1}$ and $\tilde C=D^{-1}CD^{-1}$,

$$
\tilde M R+R\tilde M^{\top}+\tilde C \;=\; D^{-1}\bigl(M\Sigma+\Sigma M^{\top}+C\bigr)D^{-1},
$$

so $M$ is an exact fit of $(\Sigma,C)$ if and only if $D^{-1}MD$ is an exact fit of $(R,\tilde C)$. The map
$M\mapsto D^{-1}MD$ is a bijection that keeps every zero pattern. Hence on both scales:

- the true model is an exact fit, with the same support $S$;
- the set of all exact fits is the same affine space $M^\ast+\mathcal N$, $\mathcal N=\{K\Sigma^{-1}:K=-K^{\top}\}$ of
  dimension $p(p-1)/2$ (the "flat fibre"), only re-expressed;
- the competing sparse exact fits (the edge replaced by its reverse, by a shortcut, …) correspond one to one.

Nothing about *which* models fit changes. What changes is how the lasso prices them.

## 3. What changes: the price of an entry, and the edge-reverse trade

**The penalty.** $\lVert D^{-1}MD\rVert_1=\sum_{ij}\lvert M_{ij}\rvert\,\omega_{ij}$ with $\omega_{ij}=\mathrm{sd}_j/\mathrm{sd}_i$.
The standardised lasso is a weighted lasso on the original scale, with the weights of the figure above.

**The loss.** The residual is reweighted too, $\tilde R_{ij}=R_{ij}/(\mathrm{sd}_i\mathrm{sd}_j)$ in
$\tfrac12\lVert\cdot\rVert_F^2$, so the design matrix, its Gram matrix $\Gamma$ and the matrix
$P=\Gamma_{S^cS}\Gamma_{SS}^{-1}$ behind $\rho$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{weak}}$ all change: writing $\Omega$ for the diagonal
matrix of the $\omega_{ij}$ over the vec index, $\tilde P=\Omega_{S^c}\,P_L\,\Omega_S^{-1}$ with $P_L$ the $P$ of the
reweighted loss. For an exact fit the loss is zero on both scales, so for $m(0^+)$ and $t^\ast$ only the penalty
weights matter.

**The trade.** Take an edge $t\to h$, the entry $M_{ht}$, and its reverse $M_{th}$ (chain: $t=1$, $h=2$, the edge
$M_{21}=\varepsilon$, the reverse $M_{12}$). Every exact fit is $M=M^\ast+\Delta$ with

$$
\Delta\Sigma+\Sigma\Delta^{\top}=0 ,
$$

because $C$ is fixed and $M^\ast$ already solves $M\Sigma+\Sigma M^{\top}+C=0$ (section 2: $\Delta=K\Sigma^{-1}$, $K$
skew). Entry $(t,h)$ of this equation is $\sum_k\Delta_{tk}\Sigma_{kh}+\sum_k\Sigma_{tk}\Delta_{hk}=0$. Sorting its
terms into the pair, the two diagonal entries and the rest of rows $t$ and $h$:

$$
\bigl(\Delta_{th}\,\Sigma_{hh}+\Delta_{ht}\,\Sigma_{tt}\bigr)
+\bigl(\Delta_{tt}+\Delta_{hh}\bigr)\Sigma_{th}
+\sum_{k\ne t,h}\bigl(\Delta_{tk}\Sigma_{kh}+\Sigma_{tk}\Delta_{hk}\bigr)=0 .
$$

*Step 1, the move.* Shrink the edge by $\delta$, $\Delta_{ht}=-\delta$ with $0\lt\delta\lt\varepsilon$. If nothing
else in rows $t$ and $h$ moves, the entry $(t,h)$ forces

$$
\Delta_{th}=\delta\,\frac{\Sigma_{tt}}{\Sigma_{hh}} ,
$$

the reverse rises by $\Sigma_{tt}/\Sigma_{hh}$ units per unit of edge: the trade rate. The ratio is not $1$ because
in this entry the edge $M_{ht}$ is multiplied by the variance of its tail, $\Sigma_{tt}$ (the term $\Sigma_{tk}\Delta_{hk}$
at $k=t$), and the reverse $M_{th}$ by the variance of *its* tail, $\Sigma_{hh}$ (the term $\Delta_{tk}\Sigma_{kh}$ at
$k=h$). An edge out of a high-variance node moves the cross-moment $\Sigma_{th}$ more per unit, so fewer units of it
are needed.

*Step 2, the price, and where the $-1$ comes from.* The $\ell_1$ norm is the sum of absolute values. The edge is an
entry of $S$ with $\lvert M^\ast_{ht}\rvert=\varepsilon$: shrinking it by $\delta$ takes $\delta$ out of the sum. The
reverse is a non-edge, $M^\ast_{th}=0$: whatever is put on it is added in full, $\delta\,\Sigma_{tt}/\Sigma_{hh}$.
Together

$$
\lVert M^\ast+\Delta\rVert_1-\lVert M^\ast\rVert_1
=-\delta+\delta\,\frac{\Sigma_{tt}}{\Sigma_{hh}}
=\delta\Bigl(\frac{\Sigma_{tt}}{\Sigma_{hh}}-1\Bigr) .
$$

Divided by $\delta$ this is the price per unit of edge removed: the $-1$ is the unit of penalty saved on the edge
itself, the ratio is the penalty paid on the reverse. Positive means the swap is dearer than $M^\ast$, as the
minimum-$\ell_1$ fit (so $m(0^+)=M^\ast$, $t^\ast\gt0$) needs along every direction; negative means the swap is cheaper and
$M^\ast$ is not the minimum-$\ell_1$ exact fit (class F). Standardised, the standardised model $\tilde M^\ast=D^{-1}M^\ast D$
solves the same equation with $R$ and $\tilde C$ (section 2), so the two steps go through with $R$ in place of
$\Sigma$: $R_{tt}=R_{hh}=1$, the reverse rises one for one, and the price is $1-1$:

$$
\text{not standardised:}\quad \frac{\Sigma_{tt}}{\Sigma_{hh}}-1,
\qquad\qquad
\text{standardised:}\quad \frac{R_{tt}}{R_{hh}}-1=0 .
$$

*Step 3, the other rows, and why this is only the leading term.* The two-entry move breaks the other entries of the
Lyapunov equation: $(t,t)$ by $2\Delta_{th}\Sigma_{ht}$, $(h,h)$ by $2\Delta_{ht}\Sigma_{th}$, $(t,k)$ by
$\Delta_{th}\Sigma_{hk}$ and $(h,k)$ by $\Delta_{ht}\Sigma_{tk}$ for every other node $k$. All of them are $\delta$ times a
covariance, so the repairs are further entries of that size and their penalty is of first order in the covariances
(an earlier version of this note said second order; the table below shows otherwise). Two facts keep the accounting
short:

- The diagonal is free. Every exact direction has $\operatorname{tr}\Delta=\operatorname{tr}(K\Sigma^{-1})=0$ ($K$ skew,
  $\Sigma^{-1}$ symmetric), and all $M^\ast_{ii}\lt0$, so what one diagonal entry gains in magnitude another loses.
- The exact first-order price is a linear programme: the cheapest exact fit with $M_{ht}=M^\ast_{ht}-\delta$, its
  $\ell_1$ norm minus $\lVert M^\ast\rVert_1$, divided by $\delta$ (`marginal_rate` in `rho_w_anatomy.py`; the LP value
  is piecewise linear in $\delta$, so the quotient is exact below the first kink: the same at $\delta=10^{-4}$ and
  $10^{-2}$).

in full). Price: $-1+1.5+0.225+0.125=0.85$. From $\varepsilon=1.5$ on the cheapest direction changes shape (it also
trades $M_{32}$ against $M_{23}$), the rest can have either sign, and the leading term is a guide to the trend only.
The exact price of $1\to2$ falls with $\varepsilon$ and reaches $0$ at $\varepsilon=2.46$ together with that of $2\to3$: this
is $t^\ast=0$, the start of F.

Standardised: the reverse rises by $0.989$, one for one up to a term of the repair's size; the repair of $(1,3)$,
$(2,3)$ is itself an edge-reverse swap, $M_{32}$ down by $0.649$ and $M_{23}$ up by $0.664$, a tie at the rate
$R_{22}/R_{33}=1$; the three diagonal moves sum to $0$. Price: $(0.989-1)+(0.664-0.649)=0.004$. Every repair is a
move of one of the two free kinds, so the first-order price vanishes as a whole and what is left is second order:
the exact price is $0.0139\,\varepsilon^2$ at $\varepsilon=0.02$ and at $0.1$, which is the $t^\ast\propto\varepsilon^2$ of the
figure.

*Step 4, the same in the weighted-penalty view.* Keep the original units and let the standardised lasso pay
$\omega_{ij}=\mathrm{sd}_j/\mathrm{sd}_i$ per unit of $M_{ij}$ (the penalty paragraph above): $\omega_{ht}=\mathrm{sd}_t/\mathrm{sd}_h$
on the edge, $\omega_{th}=\mathrm{sd}_h/\mathrm{sd}_t$ on the reverse. The move of step 1 is unchanged, $\delta$ off the edge
and $\delta\,\Sigma_{tt}/\Sigma_{hh}=\delta\,\mathrm{sd}_t^2/\mathrm{sd}_h^2$ onto the reverse, so the weighted price is

$$
-\delta\,\frac{\mathrm{sd}_t}{\mathrm{sd}_h}
+\delta\,\frac{\mathrm{sd}_t^2}{\mathrm{sd}_h^2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{sd}_h}{\mathrm{sd}_t}
=-\delta\,\frac{\mathrm{sd}_t}{\mathrm{sd}_h}+\delta\,\frac{\mathrm{sd}_t}{\mathrm{sd}_h}=0 .
$$

Per unit the reverse is $\mathrm{sd}_h^2/\mathrm{sd}_t^2$ times dearer than the edge, but it needs only
$\mathrm{sd}_t^2/\mathrm{sd}_h^2$ units per unit of edge: the two factors cancel. This is step 2 with $R$ again, as it
must be, because $\lVert D^{-1}MD\rVert_1$ is the standardised norm.

*Step 5, the rate along $\varepsilon$, not standardised.* Entry $(i,i)$ of $M^\ast\Sigma+\Sigma M^{\ast\top}+C=0$ with
$C=2I$ and $M^\ast_{ii}=-d_i$ reads $2\sum_k M^\ast_{ik}\Sigma_{ki}+2=0$, so

$$
\Sigma_{ii}=\frac{1+\sum_{k\ne i}M^\ast_{ik}\Sigma_{ki}}{d_i} .
$$

At $\varepsilon=0$ the sum is empty, $\Sigma_{ii}=1/d_i$ and $\Sigma_{tt}/\Sigma_{hh}=d_h/d_t$: the reverse costs $d_h/d_t$
times the edge, more than the edge exactly when $d$ increases along the edge. The graphs here have $d$ increasing
along every edge ($d=(1,2,3)$ for the chain), so near the diagonal every price is positive: DDK's Theorem 3. For
the chain three entries give the ratio in closed form. $(1,1)$: $\Sigma_{11}=1$ (node $1$ has no parent). $(2,1)$:
$\varepsilon\Sigma_{11}-2\Sigma_{21}-\Sigma_{21}=0$, so $\Sigma_{21}=\varepsilon/3$. $(2,2)$: $2(\varepsilon\Sigma_{21}-2\Sigma_{22})+2=0$, so
$\Sigma_{22}=\tfrac12(1+\varepsilon^2/3)$. Hence

$$
\frac{\Sigma_{11}}{\Sigma_{22}}=\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon^2/3} ,
$$

$2$ at $\varepsilon=0$, $1.5$ at $\varepsilon=1$ (the matrix above), $1$ at $\varepsilon=\sqrt3$. The child inherits the
parent's variance through $\varepsilon^2\Sigma_{11}/3$, the ratio falls, and past $\sqrt3$ the reverse is the cheaper of the
two at the leading order; the rest keeps the exact price positive until $\varepsilon=2.46$.

## 4. The same fact in the lasso's constants

**$\rho$.** Row $j$ of $P$ is the regression of the design column of the non-edge $j$ on the columns of $S$. The
column of an entry $M_{ij}$ is $\mathrm{vec}(E_{ij}\Sigma+\Sigma E_{ji})$; in the two rows $(t,h)$ and $(h,t)$ the edge's
column has the entry $\Sigma_{tt}$ and the reverse's $\Sigma_{hh}$, and everything else is a correlation. So the
coefficient of the edge in the regression of its reverse is about $\Sigma_{hh}/\Sigma_{tt}$: about $d_t/d_h\lt1$ near
the diagonal not standardised (class A exists), and about $1$ standardised. At $R=I$ exactly, the Lyapunov equation
$M+M^{\top}=-C$ sees only the symmetric part of $M$ and an edge and its reverse are the same column; standardised data
near the diagonal are as close to that as the correlations are to $0$. That is why $\rho\lt1$ never holds standardised
($\rho=1.019$ already at $\varepsilon=0.02$ for the chain) and holds up to $\varepsilon\approx0.6$ not standardised.

**$\rho_{\mathrm{weak}}$.** The entries of $Pz$, $z=\mathrm{sign}(m^\ast_S)$, are the pressures the lasso's own shrinking of
$M^\ast$ exerts on each non-edge (see the companion note, Section 3); $\rho_{\mathrm{weak}}$ is the largest. Standardised,
the reverses carry pressure $\to1$ as $\varepsilon\to0$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{weak}}=1.00003$ at $\varepsilon=0.02$), again the
tie; not standardised the largest pressure at small $\varepsilon$ is $\approx0.67$ and reaches $1$ at $\varepsilon=0.61$.

**$t^\ast$ and $m(0^+)$.** $t^\ast$ is the margin by which $M^\ast$ beats every competitor in $\ell_1$ along the flat
fibre. Standardised it is the second-order remainder of the tie, hence $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}$; not standardised it is
the first-order variance gap. This is also why the standardised R region is so wide (the second-order terms happen to
favour $M^\ast$ for most $\varepsilon$) and so fragile (the sliver of F at $\varepsilon=1.74$ to $1.78$).

## 5. What it means for the classes and for $\rho_w$

| | not standardised | standardised |
|---|---|---|
| A/B ($\rho_{\mathrm{weak}}\lt1$) | up to $\varepsilon=0.6$ (chain), $1.5$ (fork, diamond with $M_{21}=-\varepsilon$) | only at small $\varepsilon$ in 4 graphs, never for the chains |
| R ($m(0^+)=M^\ast$, lasso fails) | a band of width $1$ to $3$ before F | almost everywhere, margin $t^\ast\sim10^{-3}$ |
| F | from $\varepsilon=1$ to $4$ on, every graph | 4 graphs (from $\varepsilon=0.3$ to $2.5$ on) and a sliver in the chain ($1.74$ to $1.78$) |
| the pilot's support in R | $S$ plus one shortcut at first, later reverses | $S$ plus the reverses, from $\varepsilon=0.02$ |
| $\rho_w$ in R | $0$ in A/B, then $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-2}$, in steps | $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-1}$, smooth (the spikes are the solver, see the companion note); above $1$ for the chain of 5 beyond $\varepsilon=5.3$ |

The classes were tabulated in `../291026/part1_291026.md`; the rows about the pilot are new and come from
`results/last_segment_grid.csv`.

The practical reading: the variances of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck system carry the orientation of its edges near the
diagonal (parents vary more than children when the rates increase along the edges). Standardising throws that
information away. On the campaign's scale the lasso therefore never has $\rho\lt1$ on these graphs, and whatever the
adaptive lasso gains there, it gains in class R, through a pilot whose margin $t^\ast$ is small. Not standardised, the
same graphs give the lasso a real A/B region and the adaptive lasso a comfortable one.
